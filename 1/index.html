Restoring the Russian Empire: My Journey with Prokudin Gorskii

In the early 1900s, Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin Gorskii traveled across Russia capturing three simultaneous black and white photographs through red, green, and blue filters. My goal for this project was to take these digitized glass plate negatives, extract the three color channels, and algorithmically stack them to resurrect the original colored scenes.

Image naming convention: every output begins with out_, followed by the image name and a method suffix. _l2 is single-scale L2, _ncc is single-scale NCC, _pyramid_l2 is pyramid L2, _pyramid_ncc is pyramid NCC, and _edges_ncc is Sobel edge alignment with NCC. The page uses the exact filenames available in media/.

The Baseline: Single Scale Alignment

The raw digitizations are stacked vertically: Blue on top, Green in the middle, and Red on the bottom. I used Python and OpenCV to slice the image into equal thirds. Using the Blue channel as my stationary anchor, I needed to slide the Green and Red plates around until the pixels physically locked into place.

The Simple Idea: To automate this, I implemented an exhaustive search algorithm over a [−15, 15] pixel window. At every single (x, y) coordinate, the program scored the alignment using one of two mathematical metrics.
Metric The Underlying Math My Engineering Observations
L2 Norm (Euclidean Distance) Calculates the sum of squared differences between overlapping pixels. Straightforward and fast. It mathematically penalizes raw differences in pixel brightness.
Normalized Cross Correlation (NCC) Measures structural correlation via dot product after mean centering and normalizing the vectors. By centering the brightness and scaling the contrast, it bypasses raw light intensity and compares the actual structural patterns of the image.

Offsets Computed for Cathedral (L2 & NCC):

  • Green: (5, 2)
  • Red: (12, 3)

When I tested both metrics on cathedral.jpg, the visual outputs were completely indistinguishable. Because the structural lighting on the building and landscape is relatively consistent across all three color filters, both algorithms landed on the exact same optimal pixel coordinates. This proved my baseline search grid was functioning perfectly.

Since L2 and NCC agreed exactly on cathedral, I didn't re-run both metrics on every remaining low-resolution JPEG; I used NCC directly for monastery.jpg and tobolsk.jpg, the other two low-res images provided for early testing:

Offsets for Monastery and Tobolsk (single-scale NCC):

  • Tobolsk: Green: (3, 3), Red: (6, 3)

Scaling Up: The Image Pyramid

While single scale search worked perfectly for the low resolution .jpg files, the full size .tif glass plate scans presented a massive problem. High resolution images require much larger pixel displacements to align properly. If I expanded my exhaustive search window to cover the massive shifts needed for a .tif file, the exhaustive search would become prohibitively expensive and take hours to compute.

To meet the requirement of processing each image efficiently, I implemented a coarse to fine image pyramid. This recursive algorithm represents the image at multiple scales, shrinking it down by a factor of 2 at each step.

How the Pyramid Works
  • The Base Case: The algorithm performs a fast exhaustive search on the tiniest, coarsest version of the image.
  • Scaling Up: It moves back up the pyramid sequentially, multiplying the coordinate estimate by 2 at each higher resolution.
  • The Refinement: At each new scale, it updates the estimate using a heavily restricted [−2, 2] search window to nudge the plate into perfect focus.

The Snowball Effect (When Simple Math Fails)

When I first ran my L2 Norm pyramid on church.tif, the result was an absolute disaster. The color ghosting was far worse than anything I saw at the single scale level.

I realized this was a mathematical "snowball effect." In images like the Emir of Bukhara or this church, the images to be matched do not actually have the same brightness values because they are different color channels. Because L2 penalizes absolute brightness differences, it made a completely wrong guess at the very top of the pyramid (on the tiniest image). Because the pyramid multiplies the shift coordinates by 2 as it scales back up, a small 5 pixel error on the tiny image mathematically compounded into a massive, unrecoverable misalignment by the time it reached the full resolution.

The NCC Solution

To fix this, I swapped the mathematical engine of my pyramid from L2 to Normalized Cross Correlation (NCC). Because NCC bypasses raw pixel intensities, it never gets fooled by the mismatched brightness of the sky or water. It successfully locked onto the structural edges of the church domes on the tiniest image and perfectly maintained that alignment all the way down to the final high resolution output.

The Limits of Correlation: The Emir Exception

While NCC solved the snowballing error for the church and generalized cleanly across the rest of the high resolution dataset, I quickly discovered it is not a silver bullet.

When processing the Emir of Bukhara, NCC failed spectacularly on the Red channel, producing a massive ghosting effect. Surprisingly, the basic L2 Norm performed slightly better, though the result remained unacceptably blurry.

This failure taught me a crucial lesson about computer vision: NCC only works if the underlying structural patterns actually match. As noted in the project background, the Emir's image is uniquely difficult because the color channels represent entirely different brightness topologies. The ornate, floral patterns on his robes appear vividly in the Blue filter but are almost entirely absorbed and invisible in the Red filter.

To the NCC algorithm, these are not just different brightness levels, they look like fundamentally different shapes. Because neither raw pixel differences (L2) nor normalized correlation (NCC) can reliably match geometric patterns that only exist in one color filter, I realized I needed a completely different feature to track.

Bells & Whistles: Better Features (Edge Detection)

To solve the mismatched structural patterns in the Emir image, I tackled the optional Bells & Whistles requirement to align based on different features. Instead of relying on raw RGB similarity, I modified my algorithm to align based on image gradients and edges.

Even though the floral patterns on the robes vanish in the Red filter, the physical outline of his body exists consistently across all three glass plates. If I could strip away the colors and isolate just those physical outlines, the NCC algorithm would have a reliable geometric structure to lock onto.

The Sobel Filter Implementation
To extract these edges, I used OpenCV's Sobel filters to calculate the image derivatives (the rate of change between neighboring pixels).

  • Horizontal & Vertical Gradients: The filter scans the image matrix and records a high value whenever it detects a sudden jump from dark to bright, effectively tracing vertical and horizontal lines.
  • Edge Magnitude: I combined these directional gradients into a single magnitude map. In this new representation, flat areas of color turn completely black, and only the high contrast physical edges glow white.

By passing these black and white structural maps into my existing NCC pyramid function, the algorithm completely ignored the misleading floral dye and aligned perfectly based purely on the physical silhouette. Once the mathematical shift coordinates were calculated using the edges, I simply applied those exact same shifts to the original RGB color arrays. The result is a flawlessly aligned Emir, proving that selecting the right feature is just as important as selecting the right mathematical distance metric.

Since edge-based alignment never hurt an already-good result, I ran it as my default for the full high-resolution dataset in the gallery below rather than switching logic per image. But to be clear about what was actually necessary: plain NCC pyramid alignment on raw RGB pixels was sufficient for every image except the Emir, where edges only changed the outcome.
